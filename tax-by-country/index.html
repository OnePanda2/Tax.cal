What the table hides

Not every country hides the same amount

The interesting column is the last one. The gap between what a payslip shows and what someone actually pays is not a constant — it is roughly twice as large in Europe as in the English-speaking countries outside it.

Across Germany, Italy, France and the other eurozone countries here, spending adds an average of 3.9% on top of the payslip rate. Across Canada, United States, Australia, it adds 2.0%. Italy hides the most at +4.1%; United States the least at +1.5%.

The reason is VAT. European standard rates sit around a fifth of the price of most things, and they apply to nearly everything. US sales tax is a few per cent, set by each state, and often does not touch groceries at all. So two countries can withhold a similar amount from a payslip and still take noticeably different totals once you count the till.

Read this before you quote the table

What it does not tell you

It is not a cost-of-living comparison. One salary is converted at approximate exchange rates and taxed under ten sets of rules. What the money buys afterwards is a different question entirely, and this page does not touch it.

It is not a verdict on value. Germany sits at the top, and a large share of what it collects funds healthcare that people lower down the table pay for separately out of the income shown as theirs to keep. A rate on its own settles nothing.

It is one specific person. Employed, single, no children, no pension contributions beyond the mandatory, and spending fixed at the same share of gross in every country. Any of those changes can reorder the table.

The conversion is rough. Exchange rates move, and every figure here moves with them. Treat the ranking as more reliable than the individual numbers.

Method

Where these numbers come from

Direct tax uses each country's real 2026 brackets and thresholds, and is exact for the filer described above. The indirect side is genuinely an estimate: a shopping basket mixes items taxed at different rates, so one effective rate stands in for each spending category, and fuel is the roughest figure on the page.

Some things are deliberately left out — council and property taxes, alcohol and tobacco duty, employer-side social contributions, and the business taxes buried inside prices. Every number above is therefore a floor, not a ceiling.

Questions

Asked and answered

Is this the same as comparing the cost of living?

No, and it is important not to read it that way. This converts one salary at approximate exchange rates and applies each country's tax rules to it. It says nothing about what a salary buys once you have paid the tax, and nothing about what the tax pays for.

Why is the same salary a different number in each country?

Because it is converted. 50,000 pounds becomes roughly 59.000 € in the eurozone countries at the approximate rates used here. That conversion is a rough one, and a different rate would move every figure a little.

Does a higher rate mean a worse deal?

This page cannot tell you that. Germany appears at the top of the table, and a large part of what it collects funds healthcare that people elsewhere pay for separately out of the income the table shows them keeping. A tax rate on its own is not a verdict.

How exact are these figures?

The direct tax — income tax, social contributions, state or provincial tax — comes from each country's published 2026 brackets and is accurate for a single earner with no other income. The indirect portion is an estimate, because a real shopping basket mixes items taxed at different rates. The method is written up separately.

Who is this calculated for?

One person, employed, no children, no pension contributions beyond whatever is mandatory, and spending set to the same share of gross pay in every country. Change any of those and the ranking can change with it.
